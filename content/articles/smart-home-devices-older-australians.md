Most smart home advice aimed at older people is written by people selling something, and it tends to promise independence in the abstract while recommending devices that create work.

The useful ones share a quality: they do their job without being operated. Nothing to remember, nothing to charge, nothing to open an app for. That is the filter worth applying to everything below.

## Start with the boring one

**Lighting that comes on by itself** is the highest-value purchase, and it is not close.

A hallway light triggered by motion at night, or lamps that come on at dusk without anyone crossing a dark room to reach a switch, addresses the most common household hazard with no learning curve at all. There is no interface. Nothing has to be adopted.

Two practical notes for Australian homes. Bulb fittings here are both B22 bayonet and E27 screw, so check which the fitting takes before ordering. And a smart bulb is only smart while it has power — if someone turns the wall switch off, it stops responding to everything, which is a common source of confusion. Smart switches avoid that but involve fixed wiring, which is licensed work in every state and territory. [VERIFY]

## What genuinely helps

**Voice control**, for things that are physically awkward. Turning off a lamp across the room, setting a timer with hands full, asking for a radio station. The value is not novelty — it is removing small physical demands that accumulate.

**Smart plugs on appliances**, used for reassurance rather than automation. Being able to check from another room, or another suburb, whether the heater is off is worth more than any scheduling feature.

**Water leak sensors** under the sink, behind the washing machine, near the hot water system. Cheap, silent, and they warn about a problem that is expensive and miserable to discover late.

**A video doorbell**, so the front door can be answered without opening it or hurrying to reach it. This is one of the few devices where the safety benefit and the convenience benefit are the same thing.

**Reminders through a speaker**, for appointments and routines. Useful as a prompt. Not a medication management system, and should not be relied on as one.

## What smart home gear is not

**It is not a medical alert system.** This is the most important line in this article.

Smart speakers, cameras and sensors are consumer products. They have no monitoring centre, no guaranteed response, no service-level obligation, and they stop working in a power cut or an internet outage. Fall detection features on consumer devices are not equivalent to a monitored personal alarm, whatever the marketing suggests.

If a monitored alarm is needed, buy a monitored alarm. Smart home devices sit alongside it. The failure mode of confusing the two is exactly the situation nobody wants: an emergency, and a device that was never designed to handle it.

**It is not a substitute for care.** Sensors can tell a family member that the kettle has not been used today. They cannot tell them why, and they cannot do anything about it.

## Cameras, consent and dignity

Cameras inside the home are where good intentions most often go wrong.

The person living in the house decides. Not the adult children, not the person who bought the camera. Installing monitoring in someone's home without their genuine agreement is a serious thing to do, regardless of motive.

Australian surveillance and listening device laws vary by state and territory, and recording in private spaces is regulated. [VERIFY] Beyond the legal position, there is a practical one: a camera in a living area changes how someone feels in their own home, and that cost is easy to underestimate from outside it.

Where monitoring is genuinely wanted, motion and door sensors often achieve the same reassurance without a camera. Knowing the back door opened at 3am is usually the actual information required — not footage of it.

## The question to settle before buying anything

**Who owns the account, and what happens when they are unavailable?**

Almost every device is bound to one account, usually on the phone of whoever set it up. If that is a family member who then moves interstate, changes their email, or falls out with the household, the devices can become unmanageable — and in some cases unusable — without them.

Decide in advance:

- Whose email address the account uses
- Who else has access, and how
- What happens if that person cannot be reached
- Whether the person living in the home can operate the essentials without anyone's help

A system that only one relative can administer is fragile in exactly the circumstances it was bought for.

## Funding

Some assistive technology can be funded through Australian aged care programmes, depending on assessed need and the specific item. [VERIFY] The important practical point is sequencing: approval before purchase is considerably easier than reimbursement afterwards. Speak to the Home Care Package provider or care coordinator first, and get the position in writing.

## Before you buy

- Does it work without anyone operating it?
- Does it keep working if the internet drops?
- Does it need a subscription for its main function?
- Who administers the account, and who is the backup?
- Has the person living there agreed to it?
- Is it being bought for reassurance, or for actual safety — and if safety, is it the right tool?
- Does the bulb fitting or plug match Australian standards?

## A reasonable starting set

For most households, in this order: automatic lighting in the hallway and bathroom route, a video doorbell, leak sensors under the wet areas, and a smart plug on the heater. That covers the common hazards, requires nothing to be learned, and costs less than a single premium camera.

Add voice control if it is wanted. Add monitoring only if the person living there has asked for it — and buy a monitored alarm separately if that is what the situation actually calls for.
