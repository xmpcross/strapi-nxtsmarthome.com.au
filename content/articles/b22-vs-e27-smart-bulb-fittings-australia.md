You found the smart bulb you wanted, ordered it, and it does not fit the light.

This is one of the most common frustrations in Australian smart lighting and one of the least discussed, because it comes down to a detail most people never think about until it bites: Australia uses two different bulb fittings, frequently in the same house.

## The two fittings you will actually meet

**B22 bayonet cap.** Two small pins on the side of the base. You push the bulb in and twist a quarter turn to lock it. This has long been the standard fitting in Australian homes, inherited from British electrical practice.

**E27 Edison screw.** A spiral thread, screwed in clockwise. It dominates in Europe and, in a slightly different form, North America. In Australia it appears constantly in newer builds, imported light fittings, floor and table lamps, and anything sold as designer.

Neither is better. They are different mechanical connections, and a bulb made for one will not fit the other.

Two smaller relatives are worth knowing: **E14** small Edison screw and **B15** small bayonet, both common in bedside lamps, rangehoods and decorative fittings.

## Why buying online goes wrong

The confusion is not really about Australia. It is about where the stock comes from.

- **UK-sourced listings** lean bayonet, which usually works here.
- **European listings** lean E27.
- **US listings** are the real trap. American bulbs use **E26**, a screw fitting close enough to E27 that it will often thread in — but they are built for a nominal 120 volt supply, and Australian mains is nominal 230 volts.

That voltage difference is the part that matters. A bulb designed for 120 volts connected to Australian mains is not a compatibility annoyance, it is a failure waiting to happen. This is the strongest argument for buying stock explicitly sold into the Australian market rather than chasing a cheaper grey import.

Marketplace listings make it worse, because one product page can carry several regional variants and the fitting is buried in a dropdown.

## How to check what you have, in ten seconds

Turn the light off at the switch, let the bulb cool, and take the existing one out.

- If it **pushed in and twisted**, you have bayonet.
- If it **screwed in**, you have Edison screw.
- If there is no bulb, look into the socket. Two small L-shaped slots on the sides means bayonet. A continuous spiral thread means screw.

Photograph the base of the old bulb before you go shopping. It is faster than trying to describe it later, and any code printed on the bulb — B22, E27, E14, GU10 — settles the question outright.

**Do not assume the whole house matches.** It is entirely normal for an Australian home to have bayonet in the original ceiling fittings and screw in the lamps, pendants or anything replaced in the last decade.

## If you already bought the wrong one

Adapters exist in both directions, they are cheap, and they work.

The honest trade-offs:

- **Length.** An adapter adds roughly 20 to 30 millimetres. Inside an oyster fitting or a close shade, that can be the difference between the cover closing and not.
- **Another connection.** Every extra mechanical joint is one more thing that can work loose over time, particularly in a fitting that gets knocked or vibrates.
- **Appearance.** In an exposed pendant, the adapter is visible.

For one awkward fitting, an adapter is a sensible fix. Across a whole house, buying the correct fitting is cleaner and usually costs no more.

## When the fitting problem means you want a switch instead

If you keep hitting fitting problems, that is often a signal that smart bulbs are the wrong tool for that room.

Smart bulbs make sense where you want colour, per-bulb control, or a renter-friendly install with nothing wired. They make much less sense in a room with six downlights, because you are buying six radios to control one light.

Downlights in particular are usually **GU10** or an integrated LED unit with no replaceable bulb at all. There is no smart bulb answer for a sealed integrated fitting — the control has to happen at the switch.

Our guide to [smart bulbs versus smart switches](/lighting/smart-bulbs-vs-smart-switches-australia/) covers that decision properly, including the wiring rules that apply here.

## A short buying checklist

Before you order, confirm:

1. **Fitting type** — B22, E27, E14, B15 or GU10, verified from the socket or the old bulb.
2. **Sold for Australia** — rated for local mains, from a retailer shipping domestically.
3. **Physical size** — check the bulb dimensions against an enclosed shade or fitting.
4. **Dimming** — if the circuit has a dimmer, a smart bulb usually needs that dimmer replaced with a plain switch, because two dimming systems fighting each other produces flicker.
5. **Ecosystem** — that it works with the platform you have chosen, not merely "with an app".

Get the first two right and the rest is ordinary smart home shopping.
